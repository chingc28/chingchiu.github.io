Product economics01

Retention and LTV across 200+ purchase options

  • 200+ offerings
  • 6 billing cadences
  • 95% CI on every figure

A subscription telehealth business was making pricing and product decisions without a comparable view of customer economics. Every question of the form how long do people stay on this, and what is it worth? meant bespoke SQL, and the answers weren't comparable across six billing cadences.

I modeled retention and predicted LTV for more than 200 offerings from a transaction-grain dataset built on point-in-time status history, so later state changes couldn't distort historical cohorts. Two modeling choices did most of the work. Retention is fitted as an exponential decay with a floor, because a plain exponential decays to zero while real subscriptions plateau. And every LTV figure ships with a bootstrapped 95% interval rather than a point estimate, so low-confidence offerings can't be over-read.

Cohort-size and volume floors gate what's reportable: the tool declines to show a curve rather than fit one on noise. It also self-audits. An automated QA layer re-derives the published aggregates and surfaces a pass, warn, or fail state, and an embedded methodology tab documents grain, glossary, and caveats so the model can be interrogated without me in the room.

Outcome

Became the LTV input for value-based bidding on paid acquisition. Offering-level granularity replaced a blended average, so the signal sent to the ad platforms reflected what each purchase option was actually worth, sharpening spend allocation across the paid portfolio.

Machine learning02

Does headline sentiment predict next-day returns?

Graduate coursework · Harvard Extension
  • 1.4M headlines
  • 2009–2020
  • Answer: no

The question was whether the sentiment of financial news headlines carries exploitable information about next-day price movements. I built the full pipeline to find out: a VADER rule-based baseline, a custom Bi-LSTM classifier trained on price-derived labels (a next-day move past ±0.5% labels the headline positive or negative), and a separate LSTM forecasting next-day returns. 1.4M headlines across 2009–2020, filtered to the 500 most-mentioned tickers and merged to daily prices for roughly a million trading-day rows.

The decision that made the result trustworthy was splitting by date rather than at random. Headlines are heavily templated, with near-identical strings recurring across tickers and days, so a random split leaks the test set into training and inflates everything downstream.

It doesn't work, and the interesting part is being able to show why. Measured on the period the model trained on, sentiment correlated with next-day returns at r = 0.102. Measured only after the training cutoff, that falls to r = 0.018. Roughly 82% of the apparent signal was the model recognizing headlines it had already seen.

The classifier also lost to a majority-class baseline outright, and the forecasting LSTM matched a zero-return naive baseline to four decimal places. Directional accuracy looked mildly promising at 54.4% against a 50.7% up-day share, but across 558 test days that gap sits inside random chance. The regime shift explains much of it: training ran mostly through a rising 2009–2018 market, validation landed in a negative-majority late 2018–2020. The model learned the base rates of a bull market and then met a different world.

Outcome

A negative result, reported as one. The baselines and the out-of-sample cutoff are what turned an apparent r = 0.10 into an honest r = 0.02. The alternative was shipping something that looked predictive and wasn't.

Operations03

Daily SLA breach accountability

  • 4 analysis domains
  • Daily roll-forward
  • Dual-computed SLA math

Operations had no shared view of where orders were stuck. Fulfillment delays, medical-review backlogs, and carrier tracking gaps lived in separate event logs with no agreed definition of a breach, and no way to tell a genuinely late order from one quietly resolved by a replacement shipment. Weekly reporting was manual and numbers didn't reconcile between people.

I built a daily breach roll-forward (beginning, new, resolved, ending) across fulfillment, medical review, and delivery. Two rules did most of the work. Breaches are segmented by fulfillment partner tracking method, because partners on manual tracking structurally understate on-time performance and blending them buried the real signal. And a breach closed by a follow-on shipment within twenty days is credited as resolved, which eliminated a large class of false positives that had made the metric untrusted.

Medical review separates doctor-blocked from patient-blocked time, so the care team is measured only on what it controls. Business-day math is computed independently in SQL and in NumPy, and the QA layer checks that the two agree. It recomputes every headline number from source and fails loudly when the report disagrees with itself. That layer is what made the numbers defensible in a room.

Outcome

Adopted as the standing operations review. Gave leadership day-level visibility into throughput and quality for the first time, drove workflow changes off the tracking-method finding, and breach volume declined.

Growth04

Plan selection and the checkout funnel

  • 3 hypotheses to roadmap
  • 1 experiment run
  • +5% conversion

Checkout presented a one-time purchase alongside subscription plans. I suspected it was pulling users toward the lower-value option and flattening the plan mix.

I compared landing page variants against funnel analytics, isolated a substantial drop-off at account creation, and analyzed plan-selection mix against it. The recommendation was to demote one-time purchase to a secondary link beneath the plan selector rather than give it equal visual weight.

I deliberately packaged all of it as three sized, testable hypotheses rather than changes to ship, each documented with its supporting data and framed as an input to experimentation, not a conclusion. A mix shift worth that much is worth an experiment, not a guess.

Outcome

All three hypotheses entered the growth team's experiment roadmap. The plan-selection experiment ran and converted 5% better than control.

Also comfortable with

Graduate coursework in statistical modeling: fitting competing models on the same data and arguing about which one to believe. Two habits came out of it. Check what a suspiciously strong predictor actually is before trusting it, because a variable correlating with the outcome at r = 1.00 usually means the outcome leaked into the features. And distrust your best training score, because an accuracy of 1.000 in-sample is a warning, not a result.
